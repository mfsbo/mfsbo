# Your Business Strategy Is Only as Strong as the Skills Behind It

When organisations plan for the future, most of the attention goes to the visible parts of strategy: technology roadmaps, product plans, market expansion, infrastructure, and financial investment.

But one of the most important strategic questions is often left until much later:

**Do the people already inside the organisation have the skills required to deliver that future?**

If the answer is no, many businesses default to recruitment. Sometimes that is necessary. But constantly buying capability from the external market is expensive, competitive, and often too slow for the pace of change many organisations now face.

The more strategic alternative is to build more of that capability from within.

That is no longer just an HR argument. Research published in 2026 adds to the case that workforce development should be treated as a form of business investment: something that improves productivity, strengthens execution, and increases the returns from other investments the organisation is already making.

## Strategy fails when capability is assumed

A strategy can look compelling on paper and still fail in execution if it depends on capabilities the organisation does not yet have.

A company may invest in automation but lack the people who can redesign workflows around it. It may launch a digital transformation programme without enough managers who know how to lead through operational change. It may adopt new platforms and tools without giving teams the capability to use, improve, or scale them.

In other words, businesses do not execute strategy through technology alone. They execute it through people using technology, processes, and judgement together.

That is why skills strategy and business strategy cannot really be separated. A five-year growth plan that does not include a view of future capability is incomplete.

## Training is not a perk. It is productive investment.

One of the strongest recent arguments for this view comes from a 2026 study by **Iulia Siedschlag and Juan Duran** in the *Review of Income and Wealth*.

Using data across **17 industries in 28 countries**, the researchers found that **business investment in education and training significantly increased labour-productivity growth**. Just as importantly, the gains were especially strong when training worked alongside other organisational investments such as knowledge, processes, and other intangible assets.

That point matters because it challenges a common organisational habit: treating technology investment and people development as separate conversations.

They are not separate in practice.

If a business invests in new systems but does not invest in the capability of the people expected to use, manage, and improve those systems, it is only investing in part of what it actually needs. The technology may be present, but the operating capability around it is weak.

The implication for leaders is straightforward: the return on a technology programme should not be judged only by software selection, implementation milestones, or capital spend. It should also be judged by whether the workforce can convert that investment into better decisions, faster execution, stronger service, and higher output.

## Capability creates ripple effects beyond the individual learner

A second 2026 study makes the case even stronger.

Research by **Miguel Espinosa and Christopher Stanton** on a randomised frontline employee training programme found that training increased employee output, but it also changed what happened around those employees. Trained staff needed less help from managers, which allowed managers to spend more time on higher-value work.

The indirect benefits were substantial. The researchers estimated that the manager-side effects accounted for **around 45% of the total benefits** of the training programme.

That is a powerful reminder that the value of development is rarely limited to the person who attends the training.

When one person becomes more capable:

- they require less escalation,
- they make better day-to-day decisions,
- they create fewer bottlenecks,
- they consume less managerial time,
- and they often improve the output of the people around them.

This is one reason training is often undervalued. Many organisations evaluate learning investments too narrowly, focusing only on the direct effect on the individual participant. But the true return may include gains in team flow, management capacity, and organisational resilience.

For senior leaders, that means capability-building should be seen as a system investment, not just an individual one.

## The key word is capability, not training

This does not mean every course, conference, or learning subscription creates business value.

The problem is that many organisations still ask the wrong question:

**How much training did we provide this year?**

That is an activity metric, not a strategic one.

A better set of questions is:

- **What capabilities will our business need in three to five years?**
- **Where are our biggest gaps today?**
- **Which of those capabilities can we build internally?**
- **Where do we need to combine development with selective external hiring?**

This moves the conversation away from training volume and toward strategic capability.

That distinction is supported by a **2026 British Journal of Industrial Relations** study based on **19,289 firm-year observations** from UK datasets. The study found positive productivity relationships from training both managers and staff, as well as benefits when those groups were developed together. Crucially, it concluded that the **intensity and quality of investment matter more than simply maximising training coverage**.

That is a useful corrective for organisations that chase participation numbers rather than business impact. Targeted, high-quality development tied to future capability needs may deliver more value than broad but shallow training activity.

## Learning and development becomes part of workforce strategy

Once capability is treated as a strategic asset, the role of learning and development changes.

It stops being mainly a catalogue of courses and starts becoming part of **strategic workforce planning**.

That means business leaders, HR leaders, and functional heads should work from the same questions:

1. **What is our business trying to become?**
2. **What work will matter most in that future?**
3. **What skills, behaviours, and leadership capability will that work require?**
4. **Which capabilities do we already have but underuse?**
5. **Which capabilities are adjacent enough to build internally?**

This is also where development becomes more deliberate.

A junior employee can become tomorrow's specialist.  
A developer can become an architect.  
A team leader can become an operational leader.  
Someone who understands today's process can learn the technology that will redesign it tomorrow.

Organisations often underestimate this because hiring feels faster and more visible. But internal capability-building grows the future using people who already understand the organisation's customers, systems, culture, and operating realities.

## Recent business books are reinforcing the same shift

This strategic view of skills is also visible in recent business publishing.

David Edwards' **_The Strategic Workforce Planning Handbook_** argues that organisations should translate business strategy into future workforce requirements, identify capability gaps, and make deliberate workforce investment decisions.

Tania Lennon and Ric Roi's **_Future-Ready Talent_** similarly focuses on aligning talent pipelines with changing organisational priorities and making better decisions about when to **buy** capability externally and when to **build** it internally.

Stella Collins' **_The Learning Organization_** pushes the argument further by focusing on continuous learning cultures that strengthen organisational capability over time rather than relying only on traditional, course-based training.

Taken together, these books support the same conclusion as the research: workforce capability should not sit on the edge of strategy. It should sit inside it.

## The UK paradox: the need is rising while investment has fallen

This is what makes the UK picture particularly striking.

According to **Learning and Work Institute** research published in **September 2026**, UK employers invested **29% less in training per employee in 2024 than in 2011**. Average training time also fell from **7.8 days per trainee in 2011 to 5.7 days in 2024**.

That decline matters because it sits alongside repeated concerns about productivity, innovation, and the ability of organisations to adapt to technological change.

The UK government's **Industrial Strategy Advisory Council** has identified employer investment in skills as an important part of improving productivity and economic growth. In other words, the policy direction is pointing toward skills as a growth lever at the same time many employers have been investing less in them.

For individual businesses, this should not trigger a simplistic response of "increase the training budget and hope for the best." The more important response is to become more strategic about where capability investment goes and how it supports the future operating model.

## What strategic implementation should look like

If a company has a five-year technology strategy, it should also have a five-year people capability strategy that sits beside it.

That does not need to be complicated, but it does need to be explicit.

A practical approach might include:

### 1. Start with the future state

Define the business outcomes the organisation wants over the next three to five years: growth, automation, service redesign, product expansion, regulatory readiness, AI adoption, or operational efficiency.

### 2. Translate strategy into capability requirements

Identify the roles, skills, leadership behaviours, and knowledge needed to deliver that future.

### 3. Map current strengths and gaps

Assess where the organisation is already stronger than it thinks, where gaps are critical, and where capability is overly concentrated in a small number of people.

### 4. Decide what to build and what to buy

Not every capability should be developed internally. But many should. The key is to make that decision deliberately rather than reactively.

### 5. Invest with intensity where it matters most

The evidence suggests that focused, high-quality investment in priority capabilities can matter more than broad but shallow coverage.

### 6. Measure business impact, not just learning activity

Track outcomes such as productivity, quality, management capacity, speed to independence, internal mobility, and reduced dependency on external hiring, not only attendance and completion rates.

## Stop overlooking the assets you already have

One of the biggest strategic mistakes organisations make is assuming the answer to every missing capability is external recruitment.

Recruitment will always matter. But organisations that continually replace capability from outside can overlook one of their greatest assets: people who already know the business and could, with the right development, grow into the roles the future requires.

This matters even more when specialist talent is expensive and scarce. People developed internally carry institutional knowledge forward while adapting to new strategic needs, making transformation more durable because capability is being embedded, not just imported.

## The strategic question leaders should ask now

The most useful question is not whether your organisation values learning.

It is this:

**What will our people need to be capable of doing when our strategy arrives at its destination?**

That question changes the quality of the conversation. It links workforce development to execution, investment, and competitive advantage.

Long-term business strategy is ultimately executed by people.

So the real test of strategic seriousness is not only whether an organisation invests in technology, infrastructure, and products. It is whether it invests with equal intent in the human capability required to make those investments pay off.

Because in the end, your business strategy is only as strong as the skills behind it.
